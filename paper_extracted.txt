Powerful Multi-Model Architecture for Outcome
Prediction and Persuasive Intervention

Executive summary

Based on the uploaded project summary, your current system is not a generic multimodal AI problem: it
is primarily a streaming physiological and motion telemetry problem with optional context. The
present input set is dominated by piezo pressure-strip telemetry, ECG segments and derived BPM/HRV,
MAX30102 SpO₂ and pulse data, IMU acceleration/gyroscope, optional GPS, patient proﬁle data, and
packet-level metadata. The target outputs already described include impact detection, impact zone and
severity, oxygen/pulse risk, a synthetic time-to-emergency score, and a recommendation class. That
means the technically strongest starting point is a sensor-ﬁrst architecture that treats time-series and
tabular modelling as the core, then adds text, audio, vision, and richer multimodal capabilities behind
stable interfaces rather than making them the ﬁrst-class dependency on day one.
ﬁleciteturn0ﬁle0

The recommended design is a composed, specialist architecture rather than a single monolithic
foundation model. In practice, that means separate pretrained encoders per modality, a fusion layer
that can handle missing and asynchronous inputs, a calibrated prediction layer with uncertainty and
abstention, and a constrained intervention-policy module that chooses whether, when, and how to
deliver suggestions. For your current input mix, the best default is a hybrid of early fusion for
synchronised physiological streams and gated late fusion plus cross-modal attention for sparse
or optional inputs. This is more robust than pure early fusion when sensors drop out, and more
informative than pure late fusion when the signal interactions themselves matter. Cross-attentional
fusion has repeatedly shown practical value in multimodal settings where interactions between
modalities are important, while the NIST AI RMF and FDA/IMDRF GMLP both emphasise lifecycle risk
management, trustworthiness, and total-product monitoring for high-stakes AI. 1

For the predictive core, I would prioritise MOMENT-like time-series foundation encoders or a similar
pretrained temporal backbone, combined with a tabular/proﬁle model such as TabPFN or
TransTab, and keep strong classical baselines in the evaluation loop. For language generation in the
intervention layer, I would begin with a small-or-medium instruct model such as Gemma 2 2B/3B-
card, Llama 3.1 8B, or Mistral Nemo 12B, but only as a surface realiser or controlled rewriter on top of a
rule- and policy-governed action schema. For eﬃcient adaptation, LoRA or QLoRA should be the default
before full ﬁne-tuning, because they reduce trainable parameters and memory burden substantially
while preserving strong downstream performance in many settings. 2

The most important non-technical conclusion is that persuasive suggestions in a health-adjacent
system must be supportive, transparent, and reversible rather than manipulative. The
intervention module should never directly optimise for engagement alone. It should optimise for
outcome improvement under safety constraints, using eligibility rules, contraindication checks, conﬁdence
thresholds, escalation logic, and post-deployment monitoring. In a healthcare or safety-relevant setting,
this design posture aligns better with the WHO’s guidance on AI for health, the NIST AI RMF, and FDA/
IMDRF’s emphasis on safe lifecycle governance. 3

Recommended architecture

The best ﬁt for the current project is a modular expert-composition architecture with four logical
planes: ingestion and quality control, modality encoders, fusion and prediction, and intervention policy
with strict guardrails. The predictive stack should estimate both outcomes and uncertainty; the policy
stack should only act when the system is eligible, conﬁdent enough, and not blocked by rules or
contraindications. This split improves debuggability, monitoring, and compliance posture. It also lets
you swap the policy generator or one encoder without retraining the entire system. 4

Why this architecture is the right default

For the current sensor mix, early fusion is appropriate inside the physiological block because ECG, PPG/
SpO₂, piezo, and IMU are temporally coupled and often informative precisely because of their joint state
at the same moment. However, late fusion should still be used above that level for optional or sparse
modalities such as GPS, patient proﬁle updates, or later-added notes. This prevents a brittle system in
which the entire predictor becomes unusable when one modality is missing. Cross-modal attention is
most useful one layer higher, when the model needs to learn interactions between temporal state
tokens and context tokens rather than merely concatenate them. 5

Intervention policy module

The intervention module should not be a generic chatbot that reads raw predictor outputs and invents
recommendations. It should be a policy engine that operates over a structured action schema such as:

        • action_type
        • target_outcome
        • urgency
        • recommended_channel
        • message_template_id
        • patient_constraints
        • contraindications
        • human_escalation_required
        • confidence_required

The best pattern is a three-stage policy. First, a safety and eligibility gate decides whether the system
should intervene at all. Second, a candidate-action generator produces a small set of allowed
suggestions from templates, retrieval, or a constrained LLM. Third, a ranker chooses the expected-best
action under constraints, using context features, outcome estimates, burden estimates, and prior
response history. This is where contextual bandit or uplift-style learning is useful: the ranker can learn
which action helps which user in which context, while oﬄine policy evaluation and guarded exploration
reduce the risk of blindly optimising on biased feedback loops. 6

“Persuasion” should be deﬁned narrowly and ethically here. The system should use truthful rationale,
user preference alignment, transparent urgency, and actionable speciﬁcity. It should not use
deceptive framing, dark patterns, emotional coercion, or false certainty. In a health-adjacent context, it

                                                    3

manipulation. Recommendations should therefore be factual, attributable to observed state, and easy
to override; uncertainty should be explicit; and pathways to human review should be obvious. 3

Training and deployment roadmap

The most reliable programme is a staged one. First, establish whether the project’s outcome labels are
stable, clinically meaningful, and temporally aligned with the telemetry. Second, build hard numeric
baselines before introducing foundation-model complexity. Third, add pretrained encoders and the
intervention module separately. Fourth, deploy a conservative pilot where the system can abstain, log,
and escalate rather than always acting. This progression follows the spirit of total-product lifecycle
development in GMLP and the iterative governance emphasis in the NIST AI RMF. 44

The data package needed for training is larger than the raw sensor feed. You need aligned raw packets,
derived features, signal-quality indicators, patient proﬁle context, event labels, and intervention logs.
Without the last item, you can train outcome predictors but not robust policy models. Because the
uploaded summary does not specify dataset volume, prevalence, class imbalance, or label provenance,
the compute and budget ranges below are planning estimates rather than quotes. They assume a
phase-one system focused on time-series plus tabular inputs, with optional LoRA/QLoRA adaptation on
one text model for message generation. ﬁleciteturn0ﬁle0

A sensible default training strategy is:

        • Full ﬁne-tuning only for small encoders or when the domain gap is extreme and you have
          abundant labels.

        • LoRA for most language and multimodal adaptation.
        • QLoRA when hardware is constrained or when iterating across many policy variants.
        • Adapters/prompt tuning when you need rapid task specialisation and want to keep one frozen

          backbone for many intervention styles or locales. 45

For deployment, I recommend a split edge-cloud topology. On-device or on-gateway, keep only signal-
quality checks, cryptographic packet veriﬁcation, buﬀering, and a tiny distilled fail-safe model or rules
engine. In the cloud or edge server, host the heavier encoders, fusion model, calibrated prediction
heads, and policy runtime. If you later add an LLM or VLM service, vLLM-style serving is attractive
because PagedAttention-based systems improve throughput and memory eﬃciency for long-context
generation. 46

Evaluation, monitoring, and feedback

Evaluation needs to be split into at least three layers: predictive quality, intervention quality, and
system safety/operations. Oﬄine accuracy alone is not enough, because even a strong predictor can
still generate unsafe or ineﬀective interventions, and even a good intervention policy can fail
operationally if calibration drifts or missing-modality handling breaks in production. FDA/IMDRF GMLP
and the NIST AI RMF both point in this direction: the model is only one part of the assurance problem.

  44

For the predictive model, the core metrics should include AUROC, AUPRC, macro-F1, sensitivity at
clinically relevant speciﬁcity, Brier score, expected calibration error, and abstention coverage. Because
the current project includes time-sensitive outputs, you also need time-to-detection, false alarms per
hour/day, and alert precision at top-k urgency thresholds. For multi-head outputs, do not collapse
everything into a single weighted score until each head is individually validated. ﬁleciteturn0ﬁle0

For the intervention layer, the evaluation stack should combine oﬄine policy evaluation and online
experimentation. Oﬄine, use replay, inverse-propensity-scored estimators, doubly robust estimators
where possible, and conservative policy improvement before any live exposure. Online, prefer micro-
randomised trials or tightly guarded A/B tests over uncontrolled rollout. This matters because
recommendation systems can create self-feedback loops that hide whether the policy is truly improving
outcomes or simply reinforcing its own historical actions. 48

intervention system; they are core safety telemetry. 49

Ethical, legal considerations, and risk mitigation

Because the uploaded design is health-adjacent and may inﬂuence urgent decisions, you should
assume from the outset that this system will attract high-stakes governance expectations, even
before you decide whether it is formally marketed as clinical decision support or another regulated
category. FDA/IMDRF GMLP explicitly highlights safe, eﬀective, high-quality AI/ML medical devices
across the total product lifecycle, while the WHO’s guidance on AI for health emphasises human
wellbeing, transparency, accountability, and the management of power asymmetries and harms. The
EU’s AI regulatory framework similarly treats high-risk uses as subject to materially stronger obligations.

For persuasive suggestions speciﬁcally, the safest ethical boundary is this: the system may optimise
for helpfulness and adherence only within explicit safety, truthfulness, and user-autonomy
constraints. In other words, it may try to improve outcomes by making the right action easier to do,
but it should not exploit fear, shame, or asymmetries of information. That distinction matters more in
healthcare than in consumer recommendation, because the target user may already be vulnerable,
distressed, or physically impaired. 51

A good release standard is therefore to require each live recommendation to be:

• traceable to observed signals or documented rules,
• screened against contraindications and proﬁle constraints,
• expressed with calibrated urgency,
• easy to override,
• logged for audit,
• and escalated to a human or a simpler fallback path when uncertainty is high. 44

Open questions and limitations

The uploaded summary describes the current telemetry and output schema clearly enough to design a
strong architecture, but it does not specify dataset size, label provenance, latency targets, target device
constraints, jurisdiction, or whether the ﬁnal product will be marketed as a regulated clinical tool. Those
unknowns materially aﬀect the ﬁnal choice between a compact edge model, a cloud-ﬁrst fused model,
and the level of documentation needed for production governance. ﬁleciteturn0ﬁle0

A second limitation is that some oﬃcial model cards, especially in tabular and time-series modelling, do
not expose the same level of checkpoint detail as modern text or vision cards. Where an exact
parameter count or a deﬁnitive commercial licence was not clearly surfaced in the retrieved oﬃcial
snippet, I have marked that rather than fabricating precision. In particular, before procurement or
commercial deployment, you should do a ﬁnal legal check on the speciﬁc checkpoint versions you intend
to use, because model-card terms, hosted checkpoint terms, and upstream repository terms do not
always align perfectly. 52

The strongest recommendation, given the evidence and the project summary, is still clear: build phase
one around time-series and tabular composition, use a hybrid fusion design, keep the
intervention policy separate from the predictor, and use constrained language generation only
after you have a safe action schema and an auditable policy layer. That gives you the best balance
of predictive power, extensibility, operational control, and safety.
